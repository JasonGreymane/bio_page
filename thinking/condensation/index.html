Mould and Condensation: An Incentive Problem

  1. Opening: The Predictable Surprise (150–200 words)

Moisture risk in buildings is not mysterious. The mechanisms are well understood. The industry has decades of guidance, modelling tools, and detailing knowledge available. And yet, condensation and mould continue to emerge in particular building typologies with striking regularity.

This is not primarily a physics problem.

It is a system design problem.

  1. The Illusion of Technical Failure

Yes, condensation involves building science. Thermal bridges, ventilation rates, envelope detailing, and internal moisture loads all play a role. These factors matter, and they require competence.

But the uncomfortable truth is this: we know how to design durable building envelopes. We understand how to manage vapour, temperature gradients, and air leakage. We have standards, simulation tools, and precedents.

If failures persist despite this knowledge, it suggests the root cause lies elsewhere.

The recurring nature of mould issues in certain apartment typologies is not evidence of ignorance. It is evidence of structural conditions that make durability secondary to other priorities.

To understand why this happens, we need to look beyond the wall assembly and examine the system that produces it.

  1. The Project System: How Risk Becomes Normal

Most contemporary apartment projects operate within tight commercial constraints.

Procurement models such as design and construct compress responsibility into simplified contractual chains. Programs are often aggressive. Margins are narrow. Engineering input may be introduced late, once key architectural and cost decisions are already locked in.

None of these factors are inherently irresponsible. They are rational responses to market conditions.

However, collectively, they create a predictable pattern:

Design decisions are optimised for cost and speed.

Performance specifications are written at a high level.

Verification of moisture durability is limited.

Accountability is fragmented across consultants, contractors, and suppliers.

Durability is rarely the primary optimisation target. It competes with yield, facade expression, floor area efficiency, and construction simplicity.

In this environment, small increments of moisture risk are introduced at multiple stages. Individually, they may seem manageable. Together, they compound.

The resulting failure is not the product of a single bad detail. It is the outcome of a system that does not explicitly reward long term envelope performance.

  1. The Capital System: Why Long Term Performance Is Discounted

The deeper driver is financial structure.

Many apartment developments are designed to be sold, not held. Developers often exit projects prior to long term performance outcomes emerging. Defect liability periods are short relative to the lifespan of the building envelope. Future maintenance and remediation costs are transferred to owners corporations and occupants.

In feasibility modelling, capital expenditure is concrete and immediate. Future durability risks are uncertain and discounted. The net present value of preventing a problem in fifteen years rarely competes well against reducing construction cost today.

Insurance markets, valuations, and lending criteria have historically placed limited weight on envelope durability beyond compliance. As long as the building satisfies minimum regulatory requirements at completion, the asset can transact.

Under these conditions, moisture risk is not invisible. It is simply underpriced.

When long term performance does not meaningfully influence valuation, financing, or exit conditions, it will struggle to drive decision making at concept stage.

  1. What Would Actually Change the Outcome

If condensation and mould are system design failures, then system level adjustments are required.

Several pragmatic shifts would materially alter outcomes:

Introducing formal moisture risk registers at early design stages, explicitly identifying durability exposure before procurement pathways are fixed.

Normalising post occupancy performance audits for residential envelopes, creating feedback loops rather than relying on litigation as the primary learning mechanism.

Extending accountability windows or differentiating insurance premiums based on demonstrated durability strategies.

Integrating envelope performance more explicitly into valuation and lending considerations.

None of these measures require revolutionary technology. They require recognising that durability is an economic variable, not merely a technical one.

If we want fewer mould headlines in high density housing, we need to redesign incentives, not just wall assemblies.

Condensation is not a mystery waiting to be solved by better detailing alone. It is a predictable outcome of the way we structure projects, allocate risk, and price the future.

And that is a systems problem.
